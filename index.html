SubscriptionsProduct analytics23M transactions

Who is a music streaming service losing, and what would I change?

KKBox grew its paying base 30% in 14 months, from 904,000 to 1.18M — but that growth was running out. New sign-ups fell from 49,000 a month to 38,000 while churn stayed flat at 33,000–43,000, so by February 2017 it was losing almost exactly as many subscribers as it signed up. I modelled 23M real billing transactions and 410M days of listening to find where that churn came from.

57%of churn came from manual renewals, 13% of decisions
11%of new subscribers leave at their first renewal
3×less churn after switching to auto-renew

What I'd do: switch auto-renew on by default at checkout where most people pay manually, and test it on churn at the first renewal.

Data qualityDeveloper platform560M events

A third of the activity vanished overnight. Did users leave?

GitHub's public activity fell 34% between a Friday and a Saturday in May 2025 and never recovered. It was not users. Every event type and every kind of account fell by the same third, and no hour of any day since has gone above ~169,000 events — a rate cap on the feed, not a change in behaviour. I worked through 560 million real events on a laptop to show it.

47bad rows the standard cleaning checklist found, in 108M
37.5%of activity is automation; the usual [bot] filter finds two thirds of it
8–12×more often GitHub deletes the accounts my rules flag

What I'd do: run the completeness check automatically on any metric that moves more than 15% in a day, and report the automation share beside every headline number instead of letting it hide inside "active users".

ActivationCohort analysis1.5M newcomers

A million people a week open an account. How many come back?

Nine weekly signup cohorts on GitHub, followed for four weeks. Only 15% of new accounts ever do anything public, and only a third of those return on a second day. The obvious way to define "a newcomer" — the first time an account appears in the data — turned out to have a median account age of 1,040 days, so the first job was building a cohort that was actually new.

216/216cohort boundary checks correct, verified against the API
1 in 3of every effect was eagerness, not the action itself
1 in 5newcomers who post something get any reply at all

What I'd do: route a first-time contributor's first post to a human within 24 hours, and stop counting stars and forks as onboarding progress — they are the strongest negative signal in the data. Sized as an A/B test: 3,956 per arm for three points.

Every comparison is run twice: raw, and again at matched day-one activity — because eager newcomers do more of everything and come back more often.

Feature usageRetentionInteractive dashboard

Which features keep people on the platform?

Almost none of them on their own. Feature usage and 90-day retention for 5.9 million GitHub accounts: every feature looks like a retention driver until you compare people who were active the same number of days. Then most of the gaps collapse, and the two most-used features in the product — pushing code and creating repositories — turn negative.

+39 → +12.8code review, once activity is held constant
−2.7pushing code, the thing 62% of accounts do
19.5 → 67.5%retention from one feature used to five

What I'd do: stop asking which feature to push and aim at the second and third one instead — breadth is the only relationship that survives every cut. Code review is the single feature worth a real try: biggest matched effect, reached by 3.4% of accounts.

The switch is the point: one click turns the raw comparison into a matched one, and a manager can watch two thirds of the effect disappear.
